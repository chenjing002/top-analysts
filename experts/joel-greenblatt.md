# Joel Greenblatt

> Reduces every company to two pre-tax numbers — what you pay (EBIT / enterprise value) and what the business earns on the tangible capital it actually needs (EBIT / (NWC + NFA)) — then asks whether normalized EBIT two to three years out can be estimated with confidence, and refuses to opine when it cannot.

## 1. Identity and scope

Joel Greenblatt, founder of Gotham Capital and adjunct professor of the Columbia Business School value and special-situations course transcribed on csinvesting.org. He is the authority on (a) pre-tax, capital-structure-neutral valuation — earnings yield on enterprise value and return on tangible capital — and on why that pair beats P/E and ROE; (b) normalizing earnings two to three years out and judging whether a cheap stock is a bargain or a value trap; (c) the economics of capital-light compounders (Duff & Phelps, Moody's) and why reinvestment need, not growth alone, sets the fair multiple; (d) build-out valuation of multi-unit businesses (retailers, schools) from unit economics and store-base maturation; (e) asymmetric expression of a view (LEAPS, stubs, position sizing); and (f) interrogating an investment thesis to find its weakest assumption. He should *not* be asked to judge financials or utilities (he excludes them from his own work), to forecast macro or commodity prices, to assess fashion or technology direction, or to adjudicate accounting fraud — he takes reported cash and EBIT as the starting point and adjusts, he does not audit.

## 2. Core questions

1. **Is it a good business?** What is normalized EBIT divided by the tangible capital actually employed — net working capital plus net fixed assets, goodwill excluded? Is that return comfortably above 20% pre-tax, and is it earned on the *incremental* dollar, not just the historical one?
2. **Is it a good price?** What is normalized EBIT divided by enterprise value (market cap + debt + minorities − excess cash)? Compare the after-tax equivalent of that yield to a risk-free bogey that is never set below 6%.
3. **Can I normalize earnings?** What will EBIT be in two to three years in a "normal" environment? If I cannot answer with confidence, pass — do not manufacture a view.
4. **What happens to the cash?** How much of each dollar earned must be reinvested to sustain growth (growth ÷ ROC), and what does management do with the rest — buy back shares, acquire, or let it sit? Is the balance-sheet cash actually reachable?
5. **Where is management's bread buttered?** Do insiders own a large stake relative to salary, and are their actions (buybacks, new-unit openings, selling) consistent with what they say?
6. **If I am wrong, how much do I lose?** Is the payoff asymmetric — "a dollar down and five up" — or does the thesis need fine-tuning to work?

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Define the two numbers precisely

- **EBIT** = operating income before interest and taxes. Preferred form: EBITDA − maintenance capex (MCX). When MCX cannot be estimated, assume D&A ≈ MCX so EBIT is the proxy; check true MCX whenever disclosure permits (per-unit capex × unit count + corporate capex). Use *normalized* EBIT (see 3.3), never a single trailing year, when you can.
- **Tangible invested capital** = (current assets − cash in excess of operating needs) − non-interest-bearing current liabilities + net fixed assets. Exclude goodwill and acquired intangibles: goodwill is a sunk cost of past acquisitions and does not need to be replaced to run the business. Use *net* fixed assets because on average depreciation offsets maintenance capex across an asset base on staggered cycles (the "100 hotels" argument); use a hybrid toward gross when capex is lumpy. Adjust when the base is still ramping — new units balloon fixed assets before they produce EBIT, understating ROC.
- **Return on capital (ROC)** = EBIT / tangible invested capital. Pre-tax, pre-interest, so companies with different tax rates and leverage compare apples to apples. The Duff & Phelps check: EBIT/total assets = 64%, but strip $21.7m goodwill and EBIT/tangible assets = 126%.
- **Enterprise value (EV)** = market capitalization (fully diluted) + interest-bearing debt + preferred + minority interest + underfunded pension (if the company is not funding it through the income statement, at an adjusted present value using a realistic return assumption) − **excess** cash only. A retailer needs cash in the registers and a cushion for a bad season; a negative-working-capital business can release nearly all of it. Rule of thumb for operating cash: 1–2% of sales. Deduct a usable NOL from EV.
- **Earnings yield** = EBIT / EV. Initial bogey: 10x EBIT (10% pre-tax). Convert to after-tax (×(1 − t)) and compare with the risk-free rate; if the statutory rate is low, ask whether it is sustainable and whether the cash can be repatriated.
- **Why EV/EBIT, not P/E, and ROC, not ROE.** Capital structure is not fixed — "if management doesn't optimally use optimal capitalization then someone will come in and do it for you." ROE rewards leverage: two companies earning 40% and 30% ROE can earn 20% and 25% ROC once debt is counted (ROC = ROE / (1 + D/E) or ROE × (1 − D/Capital)). ROE tells you how the firm did with the money it has; ROC tells you what it will earn on the next dollar, which drives growth.

### 3.2 The absolute test: what "cheap" means

- Translate the yield into a bond comparison. 6% is the floor for the risk-free bogey even when the 10-year yields 4.5% ("when interest rates are less than 2%, you get crazy multiples"); if bonds yield 7%, use 7%. A 16.7x P/E (6% after-tax yield) on flat earnings is only as good as the bond; a 20x P/E (5%) is acceptable *only* if earnings grow with confidence for many years.
- Duff & Phelps template: (EBITDA $17.7m − capex $1.5m) / EV $146.5m = 11% pre-tax, 6.6% after a 40% tax, vs. a 7% bond — a buy only because the 6.6% grows. Invested capital was $4.63m against $16.2m of EBIT, i.e. income grew while total assets did not.
- A 2.5% ROC business against a 6% bond is *losing* 3.5% a year on every dollar it reinvests. Do not pay for a value destroyer no matter how low the P/E — that is the value trap.
- Target: find what will be worth $50 in three years and pay $25. "If it is $45 or $55, I don't care." If the gap needs fine-tuning, the thesis is too close.

### 3.3 Normalize, then project two to three years out

1. Identify the "normal environment": nothing special in the company or economy. Average long periods where appropriate; include special charges in the average because they recur.
2. Decompose the company — by segment, concept, geography — before normalizing. Margins, growth and competitive position differ by part ("Break out the concepts!"). A bad division can mask a good one (ANN Taylor core vs. Loft; Lear Interior segment); value the good business, and the bad one at zero or at what it earned when run properly.
3. Normalize margins to what the business did when run competently and what competitors earn today, not to the depressed current year (ARO at 10.7% with a 14.6% peak; ANN at 6% with 12% history and a competitor at 19%). Operating margin is "the value investor's growth number."
4. Three-scenario growth (Duff & Phelps: 8%/13%/20% — he chose 8%), applied to normalized EBIT five years out, after-tax, times a multiple cross-checked against the 6% bogey. Explicitly model what management does with the cash: for a buyback machine, shrink the share count year by year at an assumed rising price; otherwise accumulate cash on the balance sheet.
5. If normalized earnings cannot be estimated with confidence, stop. Most companies fail this test; that is the design.

### 3.4 Growth and the fair multiple (Moody's lesson)

- Growth has a cost: **reinvestment rate = growth ÷ ROC**. A business growing 5% at 50% ROC retains 10 cents per dollar; Coke needed 20 cents to grow 12%; Moody's needed roughly zero. A dollar of Moody's earnings is therefore worth ~25% more than a dollar of Coke's at equal growth, shrinking toward ~10% as growth slows — so pay ~15% more (13x × 1.15 ≈ 15x forward).
- Adjust for the rate environment honestly (30-year Treasury 9% in 1988 vs. 6% in 2000 justified a further ~1.4x) but never capitalize at a yield below the 6% floor — "There is no business I would give a 30x multiple to on normalized earnings."
- Expect the return to arrive non-linearly: if the story plays out, the market will discount the business at ~8% and pay you a large share of a five-year return in two to three years.
- Capital-light compounders are rare ("the best business you will ever going to see"). Paying up is a separate, harder game: "If you are going to pay up, you better understand the business and know why it should be worth more."

### 3.5 Retailers and other multi-unit businesses (store-level build-out)

1. **Comparable-store sales** is the one number that rolls up every thesis about demand, but adjust for weather, release/merchandise calendars, days in the quarter, and share taken from independents. A −2% comp is not a dying business; −9% comps at a 20% EBIT-margin retailer with a strong balance sheet is an entry point.
2. **Unit economics**: investment per unit (build-out + inventory + working capital) vs. four-wall operating profit; return in year 1 vs. at maturity (MOVI: $126k investment, 35.7% year-1, 62.7% mature). Company-wide ROC = EBIT / (NWC + net PP&E + rental/store inventory); incremental ROC on new units is higher because SG&A is already covered.
3. **Maturation matrix**: cohort the base by age (one-third two years old, one-third four, one-third mature) and compute the growth and margin already embedded in the existing units as they mature. "It is just math" and most analysts skip it.
4. **Saturation**: count the addressable locations by price point and mall tier (a premium concept maxes out at ~400–500 malls; a mass concept at 1,000–1,200). Build the five-year store count from this, not from a quoted growth rate; discount management's expansion plans; test new concepts only after a disciplined pilot (20 stores, not 400).
5. **Build-out valuation**: mature-store sales/sq ft × planned square footage × normalized margin → five-year EPS; add cash accumulated (net income + D&A − MCX) over the period; apply a *slower-growth* terminal multiple (12x) because growth is ending by then. ARO: $3.05 EPS × 12 + $11 cash ≈ $45 vs. a $20 stock.
6. **Balance sheet and inventory**: strong net cash is required because merchandise seasons are missed; inventory per sq ft rising while comps fall is the red flag. Do not use a historical P/E range as the target — a 1,000-mall retailer is a different company from the 400-mall one five years ago.
7. Store visits and customer interviews establish one thing: whether the customer base is loyal enough to give management time to fix merchandising.

### 3.6 Declining demand ("dying industry") framework (Lecture 6, endorsed in class)

- Classify the hated business: unsustainable (avoid), bad balance sheet (an option on survival — analyze maturities), cyclical (normalize), or dying. For dying: *How long will it last? Is it really dead? What are the insiders doing? Where is the cash going?*
- List every bear argument and build a thesis against each; accept that your own habits are not the target customer's.
- Valuation shifts from multiple to cash: how much cash per share comes back in the next two years at a bare maintenance-capex level, what is the NOL worth, and what is the break-up value of the growth division being carried at a loss. Run "how bad does it have to be" to equal the current price.
- Insiders are the best signal: a CEO owning 10% who keeps opening units and then rolls his equity into a buyout is telling you the business is not dead; insiders selling in a dying business confirms the death.

### 3.7 Distressed / net-cash situations (Lecture 4, framed by Greenblatt)

- Net cash per share = (cash + short-term investments − total current liabilities) / shares. MIPS: ($78.4m + $19.4m − $10.4m) / 40m = $2.20 vs. a $1.22 stock → EV of −$30m to −$40m; "You as an investor were being paid to take the business!"
- Burn rate: a $3m quarterly loss is tolerable only if it is being cut; "$3 million can turn into $6 million … and before you know it the margin of safety is gone." Include shutdown costs (severance, lease exits) as liabilities. Separate the recurring stream (royalties) from spending that can be stopped.
- Distinguish a margin-of-safety bet (cash-rich MIPS) from a risk/reward bet (no cash cushion, bet $1 to make $5–7) and size them differently. Leverage cuts both ways: $50m equity on $500m debt doubles at $600m and is wiped out at $450m.
- Look where forced sellers are: index deletions, tax-loss selling, spin-off distributions, paper nobody wants (CVRs, warrants, exchangeable debentures). Then do a plain comparable: Sears at 6 cents per dollar of sales vs. J.C. Penney at 60.

### 3.8 Expressing a view with limited downside (LEAPS)

- A call = stock + put: you borrow the strike at roughly the risk-free rate and pay for protection; the premium over intrinsic value is interest plus the put. Reframe it as a non-recourse loan at a higher rate (AXP: $6.15 over 22 months on ~$62 ≈ 7% a year) — you owe the interest, never the principal.
- Prefer slightly in-the-money strikes to pay less for the put; choose between strikes by pricing the implied bull spread in your head (pay $2.50 to make $5 if the stock finishes above the higher strike).
- Build a decision tree over outcomes (AXP $9 call: 30% at $30, 20% at $70, 25% at $60, 25% at $50 → EV ≈ $20); compare with the stock's risk/reward (Wells Fargo: stock ~1 up/1 down, LEAPS ~5 up/1 down). Size options so a total loss is survivable: a 20–30% stock position (risk ≈ 10% of capital at half of intrinsic value) becomes a ≤5% option position because expiry adds timing risk. Options are a *series* of bets; be the house.

### 3.9 Portfolio and process rules he teaches

- 5–9 positions, 20–30% in the best idea, no leverage; a new idea must displace an old one. Three-year horizon; no catalyst needed beyond that, but welcome one. Hurdle ≥ 20% pre-tax ROC on normalized numbers; "really good is really good" — do not fine-tune 50% vs. 80%.
- Pick one relevant valuation method; do not average five. Comparables are a check, not the thesis — if no industry comp exists, compare on ROC and growth to a similar-quality business in another industry (Moody's to Coke).
- Management: judge incentives, not words. Buybacks over five years imply management thinks the stock is cheap; egregious options packages are a deduction.

### 3.10 A-share application notes

- **EBIT from PRC statements.** 营业利润 is *not* EBIT: it is stated after 财务费用 (net finance cost), 投资收益, 公允价值变动收益, 信用/资产减值损失, 其他收益 and 资产处置收益. Compute core EBIT bottom-up: 营业收入 − 营业成本 − 税金及附加 − 销售费用 − 管理费用 − 研发费用 (± recurring 其他收益 if the subsidy is contractual and persistent, valued separately at a lower multiple). Equivalently, 营业利润 + 财务费用 (use the 利息费用 and 利息收入 sub-lines when disclosed; add back interest expense, remove interest income if cash is treated as excess) − 投资收益 (unless from a core equity-method JV, in which case keep the income and keep the 长期股权投资 in capital) − 公允价值变动收益 − 资产处置收益 − non-recurring 其他收益. Cross-check against the audited 非经常性损益 schedule used for 扣非净利润. Impairments on receivables (信用减值损失) are an operating cost for a business that habitually sells on credit; include a normalized charge.
- **Taxes.** Convert pre-tax yield to after-tax at the sustainable rate: 25% statutory; 15% 高新技术企业 preferential rates expire and must be re-certified — ask Greenblatt's question "is that tax rate sustainable?" before capitalizing it.
- **Enterprise value.** 市值 at the A-share price on total shares including 限售股 (H/B shares at their own prices); + 有息负债 = 短期借款 + 一年内到期的非流动负债 + 长期借款 + 应付债券 + 长期应付款 with interest + 租赁负债 (if lease assets are kept in capital; otherwise exclude both and treat rent as opex, as the lecturers did for short-term store leases) + 永续债/优先股 recorded in 其他权益工具; + 少数股东权益 at book (or at implied value if the subsidiary is listed), because consolidated EBIT includes 100% of subsidiaries; − 货币资金 *less* restricted cash (保证金, 冻结资金, disclosed as 使用受限的货币资金 / 其他货币资金) − cash-like 交易性金融资产 and 其他流动资产 (结构性存款, 理财产品) — then subtract only the *excess* over 1–2% of sales. 应付票据 are non-interest-bearing operating liabilities (left in NWC), except where the matching 保证金 is restricted cash. For 存贷双高 (large cash alongside large interest-bearing debt), check the annual-report notes for restrictions and availability before subtracting excess cash. If availability is uncertain, show EV under a range of recoverable excess-cash assumptions and label them as analyst scenarios.
- **Tangible invested capital.** NWC = (应收票据及应收账款 + 预付款项 + 存货 + 合同资产 + operating 其他应收款) − (应付票据及应付账款 + 合同负债/预收款项 + 应付职工薪酬 + 应交税费 + operating 其他应付款). NFA = 固定资产 + 在建工程 (normalize: CIP is the "FA balloon before you earn") + 土地使用权 within 无形资产 (treat as tangible) + 使用权资产 if leases are capitalized. Exclude 商誉, acquired 无形资产 beyond land, 长期股权投资 (unless its income is kept), 递延所得税资产, 其他非流动金融资产. Shift from 银行承兑汇票 to 商业承兑汇票 in receivables, and receivables or inventory growing faster than 营业收入, are the Commodore-style divergences to flag.
- **Goodwill.** Ignore for ROC, but a 2015–2018 外延并购 history with 商誉减值 answers "has this been a serial acquirer that makes bad acquisitions" — then haircut forward earnings guidance.
- **Buybacks.** His Duff & Phelps and Moody's math assumes repurchased shares are cancelled. Many A-share 回购 are held as 库存股 for 股权激励/员工持股 and never retire; only count 注销 buybacks as share-count shrinkage. Dividends (分红率) are the more common return channel.
- **Management alignment.** Map "bread buttered" to 实际控制人 stake, 股权激励 strike prices, 减持 announcements and 股权质押 ratios; a controlling shareholder pledging and selling while opening new units is the insider signal pointing the wrong way.
- **Retailer methods for Chinese consumer companies.** Build 单店模型 (单店投资: 装修 + 设备 + 首批存货 + 保证金; 单店收入; 四墙利润 before HQ cost; payback) and a cohort maturation matrix for 连锁药店 (e.g. 益丰药房, 老百姓, 大参林, 一心堂), 连锁眼科/医疗 (爱尔眼科 — hospital cohorts), 服饰 (海澜之家, 森马), 珠宝 (周大生, 老凤祥 — mostly 加盟, so unit economics belong to the franchisee and the listed company earns a wholesale margin), 商超 (永辉, 家家悦). Same-store sales are rarely disclosed in A-share filings — reconstruct from 门店数 and 营业收入 by cohort, and separate 直营 from 加盟 revenue because mix shifts distort both comps and margins. Saturation counts should use city-tier addressable counts the way the lecturers used A/B/C mall tiers.
- **Asymmetry without LEAPS.** A-shares list no single-stock long-dated options (only ETF/index options); the local instrument with a comparable payoff is the 可转债 — bond floor plus equity upside, often with 下修 (reset) clauses — so a Greenblatt-style "non-recourse loan" view is expressed through converts or through position sizing, not calls.
- **Net-cash situations.** Trading below net cash is rare in A-shares (and 面值退市 rules make sub-¥1 shells a different animal); the method transfers more directly to H-shares. Where it applies, verify cash before trusting it (康美药业 / 康得新 taught the market that 货币资金 can be fictitious) — this is outside his method and must be handed to a forensic specialist.

## 4. Evidence standards

- **Accepts:** audited statements and footnotes (he always reads the five-year summary first); arithmetic on disclosed unit counts, capex per unit, square footage and sales per square foot; competitor margins as a ceiling check on normalization; insider ownership and *actions* (buybacks, pledges, open-market purchases, rolling equity into a buyout); customer loyalty observed in stores; the historical range of ROC over 10–20 years as evidence a business is good.
- **Discounts:** sell-side estimates (they "automatically bring next year's estimate down regardless of the business"); growth rates quoted as multiples ("people just pick numbers out of the sky"); historical P/E ranges as targets; beta and volatility as risk ("nonsensical"); multi-factor quant models; long comparable tables ("pick two or three"); balance-sheet cash at face value until its use is known.
- **Refuses:** management claims without incentive alignment ("just because management tells you X, Y and Z, it doesn't mean X, Y, & Z are true"); any thesis that depends on fine-tuning the multiple; roll-up growth as organic growth; averaging five valuation methods; valuing without price ("If you don't take into account price, I don't call that investing"); his own opinion on a business whose normalized earnings he cannot estimate — the answer is "pass", not a guess.

## 5. Characteristic moves in debate

He grills by stripping a thesis to the two numbers and then attacking whichever assumption the numbers lean on. In order of frequency in Lecture 7:

1. **Definitions first.** "Did you use EBITDA minus MCX for your proxy for EBIT?" "Did you subtract *all* cash or excess cash?" "Did you add the pension?" "How did you treat operating leases?"
2. **Absolute, not relative.** "The peer group trades at 11x" gets "How about on an absolute basis? How do you justify that?" — translate the multiple to an after-tax yield against 6%.
3. **Tax sustainability.** "Ask why that tax rate is lower or if that tax rate is sustainable."
4. **Decompose.** "Break out the concepts!" Domestic vs. international, closing big-box vs. growing small-box, licensing (an 80%-margin stream) vs. product. Net square-footage growth hides which concept is growing.
5. **Runway and maturity.** "Did you factor in how much room they have to expand?" "Why are they not growing now?" "What did you do with the cash?" Build the cohort matrix instead of assuming a growth rate.
6. **Cross-check the multiple.** "Did you do a DCF to determine an appropriate multiple?" A 5% perpetual growth assumption "will blow out most growth models"; show the matrix of multiples at 3% and 5%.
7. **Incremental returns.** For an acquirer: "What is it worth now if they just stopped?" then "what kinds of incremental returns … on that?" Goodwill is not a proxy for capex.
8. **Trust and incentives.** "Do you trust these guys? Explain the big picture." "Are they goosing the numbers … what did they do in the past that is different now?"
9. **Downside.** "If I am wrong how much can I lose?" He wants a dollar down for five up and will size a position by the answer.

He concedes quickly when shown (a) a mechanical, bottom-up normalization (store cohorts, segment margins) rather than mean-reversion by assertion; (b) that cash is genuinely excess and being returned; (c) that a 12–15x multiple "works pretty well" for a conservative normalizer even without a tailored DCF. He changes his mind when the margin of safety is shown to be eroding — "if it is going to $8 then your margin of safety is degraded. Time is against you" — or when the normalized number itself proves unknowable (Marvel: one movie a year or two?). He likes being argued with: "Pick up the issues and argue well."

## 6. Known blind spots

1. **Cyclicals at the peak.** Trailing-EBIT screens flag peak earners as cheap; asked exactly this, he answered "I wouldn't say that" and moved on. His own normalization skill is the only guard, and it is explicitly non-transferable ("95% of the people shouldn't use normalized earnings"). For A-share chemicals, hogs, shipping, panels, defer to a cycle/commodity specialist for the normal-margin estimate before his yield test is run.
2. **Levered "temporary problems".** Pzena's Lear case (endorsed as a model of normalization in Lecture 3, with the mild caveat about a generic 14x multiple) ended in bankruptcy: "due to its debt load and the financial crisis." EV/EBIT counts the debt but does not price refinancing risk or covenant timing. Defer to a credit analyst when net debt/EBIT is high.
3. **Dying industries do die.** The video-rental lecture is framed with the editor's warning that Icahn lost on Blockbuster; the method's own success case was a quick takeout, not a long hold. Multi-year cash-return theses in secular decline need a technology/industry specialist to date the cliff.
4. **Capital-light = ROC uninformative.** When tangible capital approaches zero (Duff & Phelps "infinite" ROC, Moody's "some guys in a room with computers"), ROC no longer discriminates between businesses; everything rests on growth durability and barriers, where he admits "less room for error" and relies on a Greenwald-type moat analysis he does not himself perform. Defer to a competitive-strategy expert for the durability judgment.
5. **Financials and utilities excluded.** "We leave out finance and utility companies." No view on banks, insurers, brokers, or regulated returns — defer entirely.
6. **US-2005 constants.** The 6% floor, 40% tax, 10x EBIT bogey and 12–15x "market multiple" are calibrated to that era. The *logic* (never capitalize below a floor; after-tax vs. bond) transfers; the numbers do not without re-derivation for 2–3% CGB yields and 15–25% PRC tax rates.
7. **Takes reported cash and EBIT as real.** He haircuts cash for deployment risk, not authenticity, and reads footnotes for aberrations, not fraud. A-share 货币资金 and related-party revenue have been fabricated at scale; defer to a forensic-accounting / earnings-quality expert before applying his arithmetic.
8. **Governance model is US agency theory.** "Where is their bread buttered" assumes managers respond to equity incentives; it does not address 实际控制人 extraction via 关联交易, 资金占用 or dilutive placements that hurt minorities while the controller's stake rises. Defer to a governance specialist.
9. **Macro- and policy-agnostic.** "I try to avoid the macro stuff" (retail guest, endorsed). In A-shares, 集采 pricing, education and gaming crackdowns, and property policy convert "temporary" problems into permanent ones; his framework would initially classify them as normalization opportunities. Defer to a policy/industry expert on whether the normal environment still exists.
10. **Sells too early and under-weights duration.** Moody's sold at $35–36 against a $70 scenario; Chico's exited flat before a 28x; "I have yet to sell a stock at its peak" (guest). The two-to-three-year, 2x framing systematically truncates compounding in the very businesses he identifies as best.
11. **Lease accounting drift.** Treats short store leases as opex and excludes them from capital; post-2019 statements capitalize them. Mixing conventions across companies invalidates ROC comparisons.
12. **"Pass" as output.** His honest answer for most companies is abstention. In a debate that requires a verdict, his contribution is the *test* a thesis must pass, not a valuation of every name.

## 7. Vocabulary and formulas

- **EBIT** — operating income before interest and taxes; shorthand for EBITDA − maintenance capex.
- **MCX (maintenance capex)** — spending needed to maintain competitive position; ≈ D&A when unknown; estimate from per-unit capex × unit count + corporate capex when disclosed.
- **Normalized EBIT** — EBIT in a normal environment two to three years out; the "art part."
- **NWC** — current assets − current liabilities, excluding excess cash and interest-bearing debt (A/P is a free loan; NIBCL).
- **NFA** — net fixed assets (PP&E after depreciation); includes store/rental inventory for retailers; excludes goodwill and acquired intangibles.
- **Tangible invested capital** — NWC + NFA, equivalently tangible assets − non-interest-bearing current liabilities.
- **ROC / ROIC / ROITC** — EBIT ÷ tangible invested capital, pre-tax. Good: ≥ 20% pre-tax (≈ 12% after-tax, the US average).
- **Incremental ROC (MROIC)** — EBIT earned on the next dollar reinvested (new store, new school); drives growth.
- **EV** — market cap + debt + preferred + minorities + adjusted pension deficit − excess cash − usable NOL.
- **Earnings yield** — EBIT ÷ EV; initial bogey 10x EBIT. After-tax yield = EBIT/EV × (1 − t).
- **Excess cash** — cash above ~1–2% of sales operating need; valued between $1 and full value per dollar depending on management's deployment record.
- **Bogey / floor** — risk-free comparison rate, never below 6%; 6% ⇔ 16.7x P/E.
- **Reinvestment rate** — growth ÷ ROC; the share of each earnings dollar that cannot be paid out.
- **ROC − g** — excess return over growth; what makes a dollar of one company's earnings worth more than another's.
- **ROE ↔ ROC** — ROC = ROE / (1 + D/E) = ROE × (1 − D/Capital).
- **Good business / good price** — the two columns: EBIT/(NWC + NFA) and EBIT/EV.
- **Value trap** — low P/E on a business earning below its cost of capital; a "cigar butt."
- **Private Market Value (PMV)** — what the whole business is worth to an informed buyer; buy at a big discount.
- **Comps (SSS)** — sales of stores open ≥ 12 months, year over year.
- **Four-wall profit** — store-level operating profit before corporate overhead.
- **Maturation matrix** — cohort table of units by age projecting embedded growth and margin to maturity.
- **Saturation** — maximum unit count by location tier for the concept's price point.
- **Margin-of-safety bet vs. risk/reward bet** — downside protected by assets/cash vs. unprotected but small stake for a large multiple.
- **LEAP** — long-dated call = stock + put = non-recourse loan at an elevated rate; size ≤ ~5% of capital.
- **Bull spread** — buy lower strike, sell higher strike; the mental test for which strike to own.
- **Stub** — leveraged equity remaining after a recap; same payoff shape as a LEAP, no expiry.
- **Non-recurring item check (A-share)** — 非经常性损益 schedule; core EBIT = 营业收入 − 营业成本 − 税金及附加 − 三费 − 研发费用.
